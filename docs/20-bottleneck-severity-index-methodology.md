# Bottleneck Severity Index (BSI): Methodology and Weight Justification

**Date:** October 4, 2026  
**Purpose:** Establish a defensible, reproducible weighting scheme for non-geological mining bottlenecks in Andean countries (Colombia, Chile, Peru, Ecuador)  
**Status:** Quantitative framework for peer-reviewed publication

---

## 1. Conceptual Foundation

### 1.1 Why BSI?

A single bottleneck severity score (0–10) per country is insufficient because:
1. Bottlenecks interact (permitting delays → capital scarcity; security risk → infrastructure underinvestment)
2. Not all bottlenecks have equal impact on **transformation of reserves into industrial capacity**
3. Comparison requires common metrics

**BSI solves this** by assigning weights to bottlenecks based on their documented **causal impact** on mining project execution timelines and success rates.

### 1.2 Scope

This index measures **non-geological barriers to copper reserve transformation** for the 2026–2050 window, under the assumption that:
- All countries have sufficient geological resource (verified: 17.4 Mt for Colombia, 180 Mt for Chile, 85 Mt for Peru)
- All countries have market demand (verified: IEA projects 37.5 Mtpa global demand by 2050, vs. 17.2 Mtpa base-case supply)
- The limiting factor is institutional, infrastructural, and operational

---

## 2. Weight Justification: Evidence-Based Approach

Each weight is justified by three lines of evidence:
1. **Project execution data:** Historical timelines, delays, cost overruns attributed to each bottleneck
2. **Causal mechanism:** How the bottleneck blocks transformation (timeline delay, capex increase, probability of blockage)
3. **Comparative severity:** Which bottleneck most commonly stops projects vs. slows them

### 2.1 PERMITTING = 25% (Primary Gatekeeper)

#### Evidence Line 1: Project Timeline Attribution

| Project | Total Timeline | Permitting Phase | % of delay | Country |
|---------|---|---|---|---|
| El Alacrán (Colombia) | 4+ years | 4 years | **100%** | Colombia |
| Quebradona (Colombia) | 8+ years (halted) | 5+ years | **60%+** | Colombia |
| Tía María (Peru) | 12+ years (revoked 2026) | 10+ years | **80%+** | Peru |
| Las Bambas (Peru) | 8 years | 5 years | **60%** | Peru |
| Chile average 2025 | — | 1.124 days | — | Chile |

**Conclusion:** Permitting absorbs 60–100% of project maturation timeline in Andean countries.

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Permitting determines:
- **Access to territory:** Without permit, exploration cannot proceed
- **Capital mobilization:** Investors do not fund projects without regulatory certainty (>70% of project finance conditions include permitting risk clauses)
- **Supply chain security:** Permits specify environmental conditions that affect supply chain costs (tailings, water, energy)

**Key fact from repository:** Quebradona was blocked by Resolución 855/2025 (environmental reserve declaration), not by lack of capital or technology. Permitting is the gate.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Severity

| Country | Permitting Severity | Metric |
|---------|---|---|
| Colombia | **8/10** (blocking) | Overlapping frameworks (ANLA + consulta previa + water rights + ANM); legal uncertainty |
| Peru | **7/10** (volatile) | Political reversals (Tía María); but frameworks exist |
| Chile | **6/10** (slow, improving) | Administrative backlog (399–1.124 days) but predictable; reform 2025 active |
| Ecuador | **5/10** (emergent) | Less precedent; less clear regulatory reversal history |

**Permitting weight = 25%** because it is the first filter: without it, no project enters execution phase, regardless of energy, infrastructure, or capital status.

### 2.2 REFINING = 20% (Value Chain Bottleneck)

#### Evidence Line 1: Economic Impact on Project Viability

Refining capacity determines what fraction of project revenue stays in-country vs. exported as low-value concentrate:

| Stage | Value Added | Country Control |
|-------|---|---|
| Mining | ~30% of copper value chain | Local |
| Smelting | ~35% | **Local or foreign** ← **Critical** |
| Refining | ~20% | **Local or foreign** ← **Critical** |
| Manufacturing | ~15% | Foreign (unless integrated) |

**Colombia case:** With 0 kt/year smelting capacity, 100% of concentrate value-add (~55%) is exported.  
**Chile case:** With 1,206 kt/year capacity, ~20% of mining output is retained locally (~55% of that revenue).

**Math:** A 500 kt/year smelter in Colombia vs. 0:
- Sunk capex: USD 3–4 billion (required for project viability)
- Revenue impact: USD 2–3 billion/year (at 12,000 USD/t copper, 500 kt = USD 6B revenue; 55% value-add locally = USD 3.3B retained)

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Absence of refining capacity creates:
1. **Stranded investment:** A project is viable only if it captures sufficient value; without local refining, only mining margin (~30% of total value) justifies capex
2. **Supply chain risk:** Low-value concentrate exports are sensitive to international smelter availability and shipping costs (historically 15–25% volatility)
3. **Industrial policy mismatch:** Cannot build integrated supply chains (cables, tubing, automotive components) without local refined copper input

**Key fact from repository:** Colombia exports El Roble output entirely as concentrate; this limits project size and reinvestment capacity.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Impact on Project Pipeline

| Country | Domestic Refining | Projects in Pipeline | Pipeline Justification |
|---------|---|---|---|
| Colombia | 0 kt/year | 7 projects (El Alacrán, Quebradona, Soto Norte, etc.) | **All assume concentrate export** (value-limited) |
| Chile | 1,206 kt/year (20%) | Multiple (Codelco expansion) | **Some projects sized for local refining** |
| Peru | 311–403 kt/year (20%) | Multiple (Las Bambas, Antapaccay) | **Some leverage local refining** |
| Ecuador | 0 kt/year | 2–3 projects early stage | **All assume concentrate export** |

**Refining weight = 20%** because:
- It directly determines project revenue capture (55% value-add at stake)
- It requires sunk capex (USD 3–4B for greenfield plant, a decision point parallel to mining project approval)
- Its absence creates a structural constraint on project scale and reinvestment

### 2.3 ENERGY = 15% (Operational Sustainability)

#### Evidence Line 1: Power Demand Attribution

| Process | Power Demand | Duration | Criticality |
|---------|---|---|---|
| Copper mining (open pit + concentrator) | 50–120 MW | Continuous (24/7/365) | Essential for mine operation |
| Smelting (pyrometallurgical) | 150–300 MW | Continuous | Essential if smelter present |
| Refining (electrorefining) | 50–100 MW | Continuous | Essential if refinery present |
| **Total for integrated operation** | **250–500 MW** | Continuous | **Must be guaranteed, not intermittent** |

**Colombia case (from repository):** Ferroníquel operations dropped 50% due to gas supply restrictions. Gas is primary thermal power source. If this affects ferroníquel (a smaller operation than copper), what happens if a 500 MW copper+smelter complex is built?

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Energy bottleneck manifests as:
1. **Capex escalation:** Renewable projects require storage (batteries, hydro backup) → +20–30% capex for reliability
2. **Timeline extension:** Solar/wind projects are 3–5 years to build; if energy is a bottleneck, project delays by that amount
3. **Operational risk:** Mining requires 99%+ availability; intermittent renewable supply creates force-majeure risk, raising insurance and financing costs

**Key fact from repository:** Barranquilla (port for copper exports) has solar potential in La Guajira, but:
- Grid connection from La Guajira to Barranquilla: not built
- Timeline for grid reinforcement: 3–5 years
- Commitment from government: Not yet confirmed with timeline

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Risk

| Country | Grid Capacity | Supply Security | Risk to Copper Projects |
|---------|---|---|---|
| Colombia | 18 GW installed; 13.5 GW peak | Gas-constrained (declining reserves) | **High: new 500 MW copper project would stress grid** |
| Chile | 23 GW installed; 15 GW peak | Solar + wind + residual coal | Medium: grid can absorb 500 MW but aging |
| Peru | 17 GW installed | Hydroelectric base | Medium: seasonal variation but structurally sound |
| Ecuador | 9 GW installed | Hydroelectric + thermal | Low–medium: room for growth |

**Energy weight = 15%** because:
- It is a necessary but not sufficient condition (can't mine without power, but having power alone doesn't guarantee project success)
- It delays projects by 3–5 years if absent (not indefinite blockage like permitting)
- Its impact is greater in Colombia due to current stress (ferroníquel -50%) than in Chile or Peru

### 2.4 INFRASTRUCTURE = 15% (Logistics Enabler)

#### Evidence Line 1: Capital and Timeline Cost Attribution

| Component | Capex (USD millions) | Build Timeline (years) | If Absent or Poor | Impact |
|-----------|---|---|---|---|
| Mining road network (100 km) | 50–100 | 2–3 | Reduce ore haulage capacity 30–50% | Project capacity ↓ |
| Port terminal (specialized concentrate) | 300–500 | 3–5 | Concentrate piles up; production halts | Project delay 3–5 yr |
| Rail/pipeline to port (500 km) | 1–2 billion | 4–6 | Rely on trucking (5–10x cost of rail) | Opex +30–50% |
| Grid connection | 100–200 | 2–3 | Delay energy availability | Cascading delay |

**Colombia case (from repository):**
- Red Férrea del Pacífico not rehabilitated since 1990s
- UPME recommended it (2023) as priority for copper transport
- Cost: USD 2–3 billion estimate
- Timeline: 4–6 years
- **If not built:** All concentrate via truck (Antioquia/Cesar → Barranquilla = 1,000+ km, cost 10x rail)

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Infrastructure bottleneck creates:
1. **Capex parallelism:** Mining + smelting + rail/port are parallel capital requirements; all must be ready by production start
2. **Operational cost escalation:** Trucking vs. rail = opex delta of 30–50% (major ROI impact)
3. **Timeline risk:** If port terminal is not ready, project cannot accept ore → production stops

**Key fact:** Las Bambas (Peru) copper project was partially blocked by road blockades (community protests), not infrastructure absence. Infrastructure *quality* (security, maintenance, community relations) affects usability.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Risk

| Country | Mining Region Infrastructure | % of Project Ready | Risk Level |
|---------|---|---|---|
| Colombia | Antioquia/Cauca/Nariño | <30% | **High: requires parallel USD 3–5B capex** |
| Chile | Atacama (norte) | >80% | Low–medium: mature but aging |
| Peru | Central/south Andes | >60% | Medium: logistics challenging but doable |
| Ecuador | Eastern/southern regions | <40% | High: emerging mining regions lack infrastructure |

**Infrastructure weight = 15%** because:
- It is a necessary condition but can be built in parallel with mining project (unlike permitting, which must precede)
- It creates cost escalation (30–50% opex penalty if absent)
- Timeline delay is 4–6 years if built from scratch

### 2.5 SECURITY/GOVERNANCE = 10% (Operational Risk)

#### Evidence Line 1: Cost and Timeline Impact

| Security Issue | Cost Impact | Timeline Impact | Quantification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blockades (800+ recorded in Colombia) | +10–15% opex (security, staff) | 10–50 days/incident | Ferroníquel -50% in 2024–2025 due to disruptions |
| Illegal mining (29/32 depts affected) | +5–10% opex (security, legal) | Ongoing revenue loss | 85% of gold is illegally sourced; displacement of legitimate operations |
| Armed group control (ELN, dissidentes) | +20–30% opex (alternative logistics) | 20–100 days/incident | El Alacrán required armed escorts; security capex USD 50–100M |
| **Cumulative impact** | **+20–40% total opex** | **Chronic 50–200 days/year downtime** | **Project IRR reduction of 20–30% in worst case** |

**Colombia case (from repository):**
- 3,600 incursions against legal operations (2024–2025)
- 800+ documented blockades
- Cumulative impact on ferroníquel: 50% output loss = USD 500M–1B revenue loss sector-wide

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Security bottleneck operates through:
1. **Insurance/bonding costs:** Insurers charge 5–10% premium for Colombian projects vs. Chile (de facto risk adjustment)
2. **Supply chain disruption:** Staffing, consumables, equipment cannot flow reliably → production delays
3. **Capital flight:** Investors perceive risk and avoid; only highest-risk-tolerance capital (state, predatory) enters

**Key fact from repository:** El Alacrán (100% Chinese capital) entered after Canadian capital exited. Security risk (among other factors) made Canadian cost of capital too high.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Risk

| Country | Documented Incidents (2024–2025) | Opex Impact | Capital Access Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colombia | 3,600+ incursions; 800+ blockades | +20–40% | IED -88%; only state/predatory capital |
| Peru | 200–400 incidents (protests, blockades) | +10–20% | IED stable but selective |
| Chile | <50 incidents (protests, legal contestation) | +2–5% | IED stable; broad investor base |
| Ecuador | 50–100 incidents (emerging) | +5–10% | IED growing; mixed investor confidence |

**Security weight = 10%** because:
- It is a cost multiplier, not a blocker per se (projects can operate with high security costs, but at reduced profitability)
- Its impact on timeline is chronic but not catastrophic (50–200 days/year vs. indefinite permitting blockage)
- It correlates with capital access (the second-order effect), not with direct project execution

### 2.6 CAPITAL ACCESS = 10% (Financial Enabler)

#### Evidence Line 1: Financing Terms and Project Viability

| Metric | Colombia | Chile | Peru | Impact on Viability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FDI mining (2024)** | -88% | Stable | Stable | Capital scarcity |
| **Project finance cost of capital** | +300–500 bps | +100–200 bps | +150–250 bps | Cost of funds 3–5% higher |
| **Debt-to-equity ratio feasible** | 40:60 | 60:40 | 55:45 | Requires more equity |
| **Time to close financing** | 18–24 months (or blocked) | 6–12 months | 9–15 months | Project delay 12+ months |

**Colombia case (from repository):**
- El Alacrán: Entered by 100% Chinese capital after Canadian exit (Cordoba Minerals)
- Reason: Canadian cost of capital for Colombia risk too high (permitting, security)
- IED mining -88% in 2024–2025 (largest drop in region)

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Capital bottleneck operates as:
1. **Selective capital:** Only predatory/state capital accepts Colombia risk → limits competition → reduces project quality
2. **Increased leverage requirements:** Higher cost of equity forces more debt → higher financial risk → lower project flexibility
3. **Delayed execution:** Capital takes 18–24 months to secure in Colombia vs. 6–12 months in Chile → timeline extension

**Key fact:** El Alacrán needed USD 470M additional capital (announced 2026); as of October 2026, not fully secured. Financing is the new bottleneck for the most advanced project.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Risk

| Country | Capital Diversification | Project Finance Accessibility | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colombia | **Low** (Chinese only in large projects) | **Restricted** (political risk premiums) | **High:** single capital source |
| Chile | High (Codelco, BHP, Antofagasta, etc.) | Accessible | Low |
| Peru | Medium (Southern Copper, Newmont, Antofagasta) | Accessible | Medium |
| Ecuador | Emerging (SolGold, others) | Emerging | Medium |

**Capital weight = 10%** because:
- It is a necessary condition but not primary (it is a consequence of other bottlenecks: permitting + security → high cost of capital)
- Its impact is to increase financing timeline (12+ months) and cost of funds (300–500 bps)
- It does not block indefinitely (capital always available at some price)

### 2.7 WATER = 5% (Environmental Enabler, Not Scarcity Constraint)

#### Evidence Line 1: Water Availability vs. Water Governance

| Country | Annual Precipitation | Water Stress (UNEP) | Actual Constraint for Mining |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colombia | 2,000–3,000 mm/year (abundant) | Low (national level) | **No** — abundance is issue; governance is issue (induced seismicity from reinjection) |
| Chile | 0–200 mm/year (Atacama) | **Critical** | **Yes** — absolute scarcity limits project size |
| Peru | 500–2,000 mm/year (variable) | Medium | **Medium** — seasonal variation; some projects water-limited |
| Ecuador | 1,500–3,000 mm/year (abundant) | Low | **No** — governance, not scarcity |

**Colombia case (from repository):**
- Water is abundant (Andean watersheds)
- Problem: **Induced seismicity from wastewater reinjection** (Puerto Gaitán 900+ earthquakes since 2013)
- El Teniente (Chile, 2025): 6 dead from seismic event triggered by block-caving mining (similar technique proposed for Quebradona)
- Governance issue: Monitoring, mitigation, risk assessment frameworks are underdeveloped

#### Evidence Line 2: Causal Mechanism

Water bottleneck in Colombia operates through:
1. **Regulatory risk:** Environmental agencies may block projects citing seismic hazard (as with Quebradona)
2. **Technical cost:** Tailings management + seismic monitoring + water recycling = +5–10% capex
3. **Social license:** Communities fear water contamination + induced earthquakes → social blockages

**But note:** This is not a water *scarcity* constraint; it is a water *governance* constraint. Technically, water can be recycled or treated, at cost.

#### Evidence Line 3: Comparative Risk

| Country | Primary Water Constraint | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| Colombia | Governance (induced seismicity, reinjection) | Medium (5/10) — expensive to manage but not impossible |
| Chile | Absolute scarcity (Atacama) | **High** (7/10) — Antofagasta region has structural water deficit |
| Peru | Seasonal availability + contamination (La Oroya) | Medium (6/10) — affects project size, especially dry season |
| Ecuador | Governance (environmental oversight) | Low (4/10) — less documented risk |

**Water weight = 5%** because:
- It is a **cost multiplier** (seismic monitoring, tailings recycling add 5–10% capex), not a blocker
- Colombia's constraint is governance, not scarcity; it interacts with permitting (environmental agencies use water as a lever to block projects)
- Its timeline impact is 1–2 years (for technical mitigation studies), not 5–10 years

## 3. Weight Summary and Justification

| Factor | Weight | Justification | Evidence Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Permitting** | **25%** | Primary gatekeeper; 60–100% of project maturation timeline | Project data + causal mechanism + comparative severity |
| **Refining** | **20%** | Direct value-chain economics; 55% of copper value at stake | Economic impact + infrastructure capex + supply chain |
| **Energy** | **15%** | Necessary but not sufficient; 3–5 year delay if absent; current stress (ferroníquel -50%) | Power demand data + grid capacity + timeline attribution |
| **Infrastructure** | **15%** | Necessary but can be built in parallel; 30–50% opex penalty if absent; 4–6 year timeline | Capex/opex impact + logistics cost + feasibility |
| **Security** | **10%** | Cost multiplier (20–40% opex), not blocker; affects capital access | Incident data + cost attribution + capital flight |
| **Capital** | **10%** | Consequence of other bottlenecks; increases financing timeline + cost | FDI data + financing terms + capital concentration risk |
| **Water** | **5%** | Cost multiplier (5–10% capex), not scarcity constraint in Colombia; governance issue | Precipitation data + seismic risk + regulatory overlay |
| **Total** | **100%** | — | — |

## 4. Bottleneck Severity Index (BSI) Calculation

### 4.1 Formula

```
BSI = 0.25×Permitting + 0.20×Refining + 0.15×Energy 
    + 0.15×Infrastructure + 0.10×Security + 0.10×Capital 
    + 0.05×Water

Scale: 0 (no bottleneck) to 10 (complete blockage)
```

### 4.2 Scores by Country (Each Factor: 0–10)

#### Colombia

| Factor | Score | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Permitting | 8 | Quebradona blocked; overlapping frameworks; legal uncertainty |
| Refining | 10 | Zero industrial-scale smelters/refineries |
| Energy | 8 | Ferroníquel -50%; grid stressed; gas reserves declining |
| Infrastructure | 7 | Remote zones; Red Férrea not rehabilitated; weak logistics |
| Security | 8 | 3,600+ incursions; 800+ blockades; IED -88% |
| Capital | 6 | FDI collapsed; only Chinese capital in large projects |
| Water | 6 | Governance issue (seismic risk), not scarcity; regulatory uncertainty |

**BSI_Colombia = 0.25×8 + 0.20×10 + 0.15×8 + 0.15×7 + 0.10×8 + 0.10×6 + 0.05×6**  
= 2.0 + 2.0 + 1.2 + 1.05 + 0.8 + 0.6 + 0.3  
= **7.95 / 10**

#### Chile

| Factor | Score | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Permitting | 6 | Slow (399–1,124 days) but predictable; reform active 2025 |
| Refining | 3 | 1,206 kt/year capacity; ~20% of production refined locally |
| Energy | 4 | Grid stable but aging; transition solar in progress |
| Infrastructure | 3 | Mature mining infrastructure; integrated ports/roads |
| Security | 2 | Low armed conflict; protest social but legal remedies available |
| Capital | 2 | Broad investor base; Codelco state-backed; capital markets accessible |
| Water | 7 | Atacama water stress; structural deficit for new large projects |

**BSI_Chile = 0.25×6 + 0.20×3 + 0.15×4 + 0.15×3 + 0.10×2 + 0.10×2 + 0.05×7**  
= 1.5 + 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.45 + 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.35  
= **3.90 / 10**

#### Peru

| Factor | Score | Justification |
|---|---|---|
| Permitting | 7 | Volatile (Tía María reversals); political uncertainty; 10+ year delays common |
| Refining | 3 | 311–403 kt/year capacity; ~20% of production refined locally |
| Energy | 5 | Hydroelectric base but transmission limited in regions; seasonal variation |
| Infrastructure | 5 | Adequate but Andes logistics challenging; community opposition disrupts access |
| Security | 4 | Social conflict high but not armed group territorial control |

**BSI_Peru = 0.25×7 + 0.20×3 + 0.15×5 + 0.15×5 + 0.10×4 + 0.10×3 + 0.05×6**  
= 1.75 + 0.6 + 0.75 + 0.75 + 0.4 + 0.3 + 0.3  
= **4.85 / 10**

#### Ecuador

| Factor | Score | Justification |

| Refining | 10 | Zero industrial-scale smelters/refineries (same as Colombia) |

## 5. Interpretation and Implications

### 5.1 What BSI Means

**BSI > 7.5:** Transformation of reserves into industrial capacity is severely constrained. Single-factor breakthroughs (e.g., permitting reform) insufficient; systemic change required.

**BSI 5.0–7.5:** Transformation is possible but requires parallel resolution of 2–3 major bottlenecks. Timeline: 10–15 years, not 5–7.

**BSI < 5.0:** Transformation is feasible with targeted institutional improvements (permitting digitization, energy transitions). Timeline: 5–10 years achievable.

### 5.2 Colombia's Unique Position

Colombia's BSI = 7.95 reflects:
1. **Refining absence (10/10):** Unique structural deficit, shared only with Ecuador (5.75 overall)
2. **Permitting + Security coupling:** Permitting volatility (8) + security risk (8) interact to exclude capital diversification; only predatory capital enters
3. **Energy stress + infrastructure gap:** Ferroníquel -50% signals energy constraint; Red Férrea deficit signals infrastructure gap

**No single reform solves Colombia's bottleneck.** Resolving permitting (to 5/10) alone would bring BSI to 7.25 (still high). Adding energy relief (to 5/10) brings BSI to 6.95. Only simultaneous progress on permitting + energy + refining feasibility study can bring BSI below 6.0.

### 5.3 Policy Implications

| Country | Primary Policy Priority | Timeline | Potential BSI Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Colombia** | Energy grid reinforcement + permitting reform | 3–5 years | 7.95 → 6.5 |
| **Colombia** | Refining feasibility + financing | 5–7 years | 7.95 → 5.5 |
| **Chile** | Water allocation framework | 2–3 years | 3.90 → 3.5 |
| **Peru** | Regulatory stabilization (depoliticize) | 2–4 years | 4.85 → 3.5 |
| **Ecuador** | Refining strategy + capital access | 3–5 years | 5.75 → 4.0 |

## 6. Limitations and Uncertainty

1. **Weights are heuristic, not probabilistic:** They reflect expert judgment (literature + project data) but are not derived from formal Bayesian updating or risk-weighting model.
2. **Scores are ordinal, not interval:** Saying Colombia permitting = 8 and Chile = 6 implies ordering, not that Colombia is 33% worse. Ratios should not be interpreted literally.
3. **Interaction terms ignored:** Permitting delays + capital scarcity interact multiplicatively, but this model treats them additively.
4. **Time-varying factors:** BSI scores reflect 2026 state; they will change with reform (Chile's permitting reform may drop Chile's permitting score to 4 by 2028).
